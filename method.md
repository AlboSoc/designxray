# The PPPP Method

## Purpose

Ask what job the feature performs. Include more than mechanical function:

- structural support;
- comfort or ergonomics;
- signalling and legibility;
- safety;
- manufacturing convenience;
- maintenance access;
- deterrence or control;
- atmosphere and social behaviour;
- cost reduction;
- compatibility with standards or neighbouring components.

A feature may serve several purposes, and an apparent purpose may be secondary to a manufacturing or maintenance need.

## Pressures

Reconstruct the forces that could have shaped the design:

- loads, friction, vibration, weather, heat, fluids, dirt, and wear;
- material properties and available processes;
- tooling, assembly sequence, tolerances, and repair;
- cost, supply chain, shipping, storage, and labour;
- regulation, accessibility, hygiene, fire safety, and liability;
- convention, expectation, fashion, branding, and legibility;
- misuse, vandalism, theft, and operator error;
- constraints inherited from older systems.

Look for compromises. A conspicuous shape often reflects several pressures at once.

## Perils

Identify credible ways the design can go wrong:

- breakage, loosening, fatigue, corrosion, staining, snagging, tipping;
- confusing affordances or hidden controls;
- awkward cleaning, maintenance, replacement, or inspection;
- accessibility failures;
- pinch points, sharp edges, trip hazards, overheating, contamination;
- local optimisation that creates a system-level problem;
- incentives for misuse;
- ageing poorly or becoming obsolete;
- failure under unusual users, environments, or loads.

A peril should follow from the feature’s actual form or context, not be generic pessimism.

## Possibilities

Generate grounded alternatives:

- remove, combine, relocate, enlarge, shrink, soften, expose, conceal;
- change material, geometry, joining method, surface, or orientation;
- make wear visible or make repair easier;
- make behaviour adjustable, reversible, modular, or replaceable;
- transfer a useful principle to another domain;
- reinterpret an accidental feature as a deliberate capability;
- propose a small experiment that would distinguish competing explanations.

Possibilities should emerge from Purpose, Pressures, and Perils rather than becoming free-floating brainstorming.

## Evidence discipline

Use three levels:

- **Observation:** directly visible or explicitly supplied by the user.
- **Inference:** a plausible explanation supported by visible evidence.
- **Speculation:** an interesting but weakly supported possibility.

Useful phrasing includes:

- “Visible in the image…”
- “This likely…”
- “One plausible reason is…”
- “An alternative explanation is…”
- “More speculatively…”

## Feature selection

Prioritise details with high explanatory leverage:

- interfaces and joints;
- transitions between materials;
- repeated components;
- asymmetries;
- adjustment mechanisms;
- guards, stops, lips, caps, ribs, chamfers, recesses, and overhangs;
- evidence of wear or repair;
- features whose purpose is not immediately obvious;
- absences that constrain behaviour;
- relationships between objects and spatial layout.

## Synthesis

After individual analyses, ask:

- Which pressures recur?
- What is optimised: cost, durability, cleaning, throughput, atmosphere, flexibility?
- Where has complexity been moved?
- Which user is implicitly assumed?
- What kinds of misuse are anticipated?
- Is the system designed for graceful degradation?
- Which features exist mainly because of another feature?
- What does wear reveal that pristine design does not?
